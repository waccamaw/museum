Every other exhibit in this museum looks back. This one does not. Chief Hatcher built
a tribe meant to outlast him, and on **June 29, 2026** it did exactly that: the
chieftaincy passed, cleanly and by the tribe's own law, to **Chief Cheryl
Cail** — and the Waccamaw Indian People carry on.

## A planned succession, not a rupture

Buster Hatcher's last act as Chief was to make sure the handoff was orderly. He had
already **stepped back over his final year** to let his Vice Chief lead, so that when
he resigned, nothing lurched. Under **Article VII** of the 2016 Constitution, the
office passes automatically from Chief to Vice Chief — and so it did. He kept only the
lifetime title, and his blessing:

> "Please give your loyalty to Chief Cail, I believe she will lead ably and honestly."
> — *Chief Hatcher's resignation letter, June 29, 2026*

## Chief Cheryl Cail

She did not come to this suddenly. For years she served as **Vice Chief** at Chief
Hatcher's side, and long before that she had made the same journey home that so many
Waccamaw describe — from not knowing, to knowing, to belonging. In her own words, from
the *Waccamaw Voices* oral histories:

> "Knowing that you're Indian, knowing that you have been told this all your life, but
> not having any information … when I learned more, it was like finding a home."
> — *Vice Chief Cheryl Cail, Coastal Carolina University oral history, 2021*

It is nearly the same sentence Chief Hatcher used about the day a Cherokee woman
treated a small, out-of-place boy as one of her own — *"I have a place to be now."*
Two chiefs, one thesis: the tribe is a home for people who were told they had none.

## The government carries on

The Waccamaw are not one leader; they are an institution built to endure. Under the
2016 Constitution the tribe governs itself through **three branches**, an elected
**Tribal Council**, a **Council of Elders**, and the officers of tribal government —
the same machinery Chief Hatcher spent thirty years building, now in new hands. The
day-to-day work of the tribe — the roll, the meetings, the ceremonies — did not pause
for the change of command.

## Still here

The Pauwau still gathers each year at the Tribal Grounds in Dog Bluff. **Waccamaw
Day** is still kept. And the fight Chief Hatcher never finished — **federal
recognition**, and with it the right to bring the tribe's ancestors home — is now the
work of the administration that follows him. The phrase the community uses for all of
it is the plainest true thing the museum can end on:

> **#WeAreStillHere.**
