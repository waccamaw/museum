Governance archives sound dry until you notice what a tribe actually legislates
*about*. Between the budgets and the bylaws, the Waccamaw record keeps writing its
culture into law:

**A storyteller, by resolution.** In 2006 the tribe formally designated its
storyteller (JHH-10-06-2006-001) — an oral-tradition office created by written
instrument, which is the whole modern tribal condition in one document.

**Waccamaw Day.** Established by resolution in 2009 (JHH-07-10-2009-001) — the
tribe's own holiday, on its own calendar, by its own law.

**The Pauwau, year-round.** In 2006–07 the Council converted the Pauwau (pow-wow)
Committee from a per-event crew into a standing, year-round body with its own tribal
sub-account (SC-12-01-2006-001) — the paperwork shape of a festival that had outgrown
its weekend. The signed original carries a hand-struck word in the operative clause;
the archive preserves the emendation verbatim, because on documents like these the
handwriting *is* history.

**The Woccon language.** The archive carries a standing committee's files on Woccon —
the Catawban language of the Waccamaw's ancestors, recorded in a 1709 wordlist and
long dormant. That there is a *committee folder* for it in a working tribal archive
says more about persistence than any essay could.

**Memorial trees, heritage months, youth weekends** — the record runs right down to a
policy on memorial plantings and the proclamation for Native American Indian Heritage
Month. Small laws, deliberately kept.

Culture is what a people does; the archive is where a people admits in writing that
it means to keep doing it.
