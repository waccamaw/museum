Ask Chief Hatcher where the Waccamaw came from and he would take you back two
centuries, to a bend in the land near Dog Bluff, in Horry County — to the **Dimery
Settlement.** It is the ground the modern tribe grew from.

## 300 acres, bought back

> "In 1813 a fella named John Dimery … gathered together a bunch of his friends,
> collected some money, and they bought 300 acres of land back from Mr. Lewis. And
> that land is what became the Dimery Settlement." — *Chief Harold Hatcher, oral
> history, 2014*

In the early 1800s, **John Dimery and his wife Elizabeth** purchased those 300 acres
near Dog Bluff. They were soon joined by other families — **Thompkins, Cook, Turner,
Cooper, Hatcher, Elvis** — and together they formed a small, insular farming
community that would hold together for the better part of a century, with its own
**two churches and a school.**

The census takers had no box for them. They were recorded as *"mulatto"* — a
catch-all the era used for people who were neither White nor enslaved — and the
county's own newspaper struggled with the same question. The *Horry Herald* wrote, on
**April 23, 1921:**

> "They are mixed as to race, claiming that they have Indian blood in their veins."

## The schoolhouse, and the fight that ran in the blood

Being *neither Black nor White* was not a curiosity in the segregated South — it was
a crisis, and it came for the settlement's children. There was no school the county
would let them attend. In **1923**, a proposition was floated to set aside a
schoolhouse for them alone. The *Horry Herald*, **October 18, 1923:**

> "There was a proposition made … to set apart what is known as the Pine Level School
> in the Rehobeth District as a school which could be attended by the Dimery people
> and their relatives, and by people of the like kind from other school districts in
> that part of Horry County."

That was the **Pine Level School** — the "Dimery School." A dozen years later, in
**1935**, the county tried to fold the Indian children into the "colored" school
instead. Chief Hatcher's grandfather refused:

> "My granddaddy — his name was **Vander Hatcher** — … took them all to the white
> school, and they wouldn't let them in. … a group of white parents came out, beat
> the kids, and ran them away. So … my granddaddy sued the county." — *Chief Hatcher,
> oral history, 2014*

The county's answer was to sort the settlement's children one by one, by the shade of
their skin:

> "One of my uncles went to the black school, and his sister went to the white
> school. **Same mother, same father.**"

It is the oldest chapter of the same argument the tribe is still making — that the
Waccamaw are here, and always were.

## From settlement to tribe

The Dimery Settlement never scattered. "Even today," Chief Hatcher said, "there's a
lot of Indians living in and around that area." When the modern tribe organized in
**1992** and later acquired its **20 acres of Tribal Grounds at Dog Bluff (2004)**,
it was coming home to the same ground John Dimery bought back in 1813 — and the
**Pauwau** and **Waccamaw Day** are held there still.
