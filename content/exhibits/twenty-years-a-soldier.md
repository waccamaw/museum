*On June 27, 2006, Wendy Carmen Trott of the National Association of Native American
Studies — an official partner of the Library of Congress Veterans History Project —
sat down with Chief Hatcher in Conway and asked him about his twenty years in the
Army. The museum has long quoted the memoir he typed for the same collection; this
exhibit opens the interview itself, newly transcribed in full — 7,477 words of him
talking. Everything below in quotation is his, verbatim as spoken, with elisions
marked and the transcript's occasional mishearing set right in [brackets]. The
italics are the museum's only additions.*

> "I went in the Army on February 5th, 1968 and got out on March 1st, 1988. So I
> served 20 years."

## Ninety dollars a month

*He was a ninth-grade dropout working for wages that couldn't feed him, living with
his sister in Charlotte, with a brother already in Vietnam.*

> "I quit school with a ninth grade and worked at a place here called The Charcoal
> Plant. I earned $2.10 an hour I think it was. Went to live with my sister in
> Charlotte and was working at Lance Incorporated making $2.60 something cents an
> hour, didn't figure I could live on that so I joined the Army to make $90 a month."

> "Well my brother was in Vietnam We were real close when we grew up. I just kind of
> wanted to do something for the country and see my brother, but at that time all you
> had to do was live and breathe And the Army would take you … back in those days it
> was just put a stamp on you and send you to Vietnam"

*Asked why the Army and not some other branch:*

> "Yeah, I was driving by it and saw the sign outside the street and said, **I think
> I'll join the Army.**"

## Basic

> "There's something that kind of stuck in my mind was when we went into the basic
> training they cut everybody's hair off And that's the first time I've seen a grown
> man cry when they cut their hair off. It just really amazes me that people were
> that attached to their hair, but I just thought it was funny."

> "My drill sergeant was a fellow named Lucas. I don't know his first name. He's a
> little teeny man but he was an expert in hand-to-hand combat … He was pretty tough
> and I was happy to have him there because I knew we were going to Vietnam and I
> wanted to learn as best I could how to take care of myself So I'll always remember
> [Drill] Sergeant Lucas."

## In country

*He landed in a combat zone and waited days to be handed a rifle.*

> "I kind of thought it was really dumb because we were in a combat zone, and we got
> off the airplane. It was like two or three days before we got our weapon. And I
> thought that was kind of dumb."

> "At night when you'd be trying to sleep or something the mortars would start coming
> in … It scared the daylights out of me because you could hear mortars and that kind
> of stuff coming through the air. That sounded like whoo-whoo-whoo! And no matter
> where you are you think it's coming at you"

> "First time I believe I ever saw a dead body that was not in the casket was coming
> down the river, and that was scary too"

*His permanent unit was cavalry reconnaissance of the 82nd Airborne — he rode an
armored personnel carrier as an M60 machine gunner.*

> "We got into a lot more than that later on after I got to my permanent unit which
> was the 82nd Airborne Division even in Vietnam. The B Troop 1st [of the] 17th
> Cavalry 3rd Brigade of the 82d Airborne Division Some of the meanest people in the
> world I believe were hardcore people and I think thats probably made me as hard as
> I am today, which is not really all that good."

## "They beg They cry"

*Content note: this section and the next carry the war whole — the dying, and the
killing of two allied soldiers at a night checkpoint.*

*Asked whether his unit took many casualties, he refused the movie version:*

> "If they look at television and you see an individual get shot and he just falls
> over … That's not real combat. Sylvester Stallone with his .45 caliber pistol could
> shoot a jet plane down and they couldn't hit him with a mini gun In reality people
> get shot all around us And they don't just die **They beg They cry** things that you
> never forget. These young men, I was 18 years old in combat and my kid turned 18
> and I'd be afraid for him to drive a car half the time."

> "You never knew who your enemy was. In World War I and World War II, you had the
> lines of battle … In Vietnam that wasn't the case. You could see a child walking up
> to you and he could be loaded with explosives"

*One night at a defensive position near a crossroads, a Vietnamese truck came up
loud and fast in the dark:*

> "The lieutenant trying to get things calmed down pulled out his .45 caliber pistol
> and shot it into the air. When he did people just opened up on the truck. They just
> tore these two men up but it was two South Vietnamese soldiers just trying to get
> through the check site."

*What he carried for protection was not a charm:*

> "I played gospel music on my guitar. Now, I'm not a Christian now but it gave me
> comfort to play gospel music back in those days"

## The tunnel

*The tribe knows the story from his memoir; here he told it out loud. Three-man
teams, pistols and one flashlight, multiple levels down. The lead man was shot on
the third level; the man behind him ran.*

> "I received a Bronze Star … for dragging him out of the tunnel. Of course he was
> dead when I got him out but I didn't know that at the time we were in the tunnel …
> And the guy behind me who was the guy who had the light ran We got fired on over
> there He just took off and left me with the other guy … It wasn't like I was trying
> to be a hero. It was like, I just thought that I wanted him with me. You know? I
> didn't want him by myself or something. More for me than him. More selfish but they
> thought it was some act of [valor] and gave me a Bronze Star"

*Eighteen years earlier, typing the same story for the same archive, he had insisted
on the same point, in the same plain hand:*

> "It wasnt that I was trying to save his life, **It wasn't bravery.** I don't know
> what it was but they gave me a Bronze star for it."
> — *from his memoir, in his own hand, same collection*

## Three bullets

*He was on night guard in an area designated safe, playing guitar and smoking. He
pointed to the places as he talked — the arm, the side, the belly.*

> "No it was all in one incident I was playing guitar and smoking a cigarette at that
> time I smoked … And the rounds started coming in … And I started to jump and when I
> did, I got caught here in the arm. And once here in the side and then once right
> here just went on the ground."

> "I remember waking up on the helicopter being flown in and I thought that I was on
> the outside of the … chopper … I woke up with my head hanging like that and seeing
> trees way down below me and it scared me"

> "I never saw anybody from my unit but I did wake up one morning had a lot of medals
> pinned to my pillow"

## Scott Air Force Base

*The flight home from Japan stopped in Illinois. He was carried off on a stretcher.*

> "I remember being unloaded on a stretcher and people standing outside the fence
> calling us baby killers and stuff like that trying to spit at us. And they …
> promised us a free steak and a free phone call home. And I had tubes and all that
> in there couldn't eat steaks"

> "I called my mother and told her that I was supposed to be landing in Fort Gordon
> Hospital … when I got there she was there."

## "If you're not white you're not right"

*Content note: the next passage quotes, in his own dismissive retelling, the
anti-Indian slur soldiers hung on him.*

*Asked whether he was ever treated differently for his race, he said the Army was
the fairest place he'd been — and then told the truth sideways:*

> "During the military people used to call me blanket ass because that was their pet
> term for an Indian but they didn't despise Indians it was just a joke to them"

> "Like the memorial in Washington D.C., about Vietnam Not The Black Wall but the
> other memorial where most people in Vietnam were people of color But if you go to
> … this memorial there's only one person of color there and the rest of them are
> white. **It seems like if you're not white you're not right** and that's the way it
> goes but I think that's just an attitude … a general attitude in this country that
> way."

*And the Army's censors gave him a question he never stopped asking. The one USO
show he saw in Vietnam wasn't allowed to be a striptease; the famous photograph of
the burned girl ran with her wounds raw and her body blacked out.*

> "It was all censored. I just thought that was kind of strange that the Army was
> looking after our moral values and you wonder what really is immoral? Is it taking
> a man's head off or watching a woman naked?"

> "They didn't blacken out the ugly horror of the war by showing the burns and all
> that. That was all raw. But they blackened out her breast and her genitalia. You
> wonder what our standards are really?"

## "An educated dummy"

*His wounds ended his infantry days; he retrained as a mechanic, rose from E-4
through the enlisted ranks, and won a direct appointment to warrant officer. Along
the way he built a whole theory of rank, learned the hard way:*

> "I felt like a second lieutenant was dangerous … not because they're stupid but
> because there would be a college kid one day and in charge of a platoon In combat,
> they are dangerous because they don't know. They got seasoned E-7s and E-6s out
> there who's been in combat before … And some of these guys don't understand that
> experience is more important than education. See the Army has always thought that
> … education makes mark but education doesn't make mark. **You can educate a dummy
> and you've got an educated dummy** But the Army never did figure that out, I don't
> think."

*He had the proof on his own body:*

> "When I was shot for example, I couldn't breathe … And I thought I was going to
> die from not being able to breathe. That lieutenant was standing there holding my
> shoulders. Why he was holding my shoulders? I don't know. He was my platoon
> leader. And an E-4 came up and sat me up and started making me breathe … I never
> will forget that either … The guy that was in charge, that should have known what
> to do didn't have any idea what to do."

*But the dummy line was never against learning — he was its best argument the other
way:*

> "I went into the military with a ninth grade education but I got out with a degree
> and I put in a lot of effort after hours to make that happen. And I think that's
> the key for folks nowadays is you've gotta have something to hang on the wall no
> matter how smart you are"

## Eleven years at Bragg, twenty in all

> "I spent 11 years with the 82nd at Fort Bragg but these guys are crazy! … you
> could go down to the supply room and they'd take a stapler … And they would staple
> your shirt to your chest. Stuff like that. You couldn't turn your back on anybody.
> People were just crazy! I guess they had to be that way … because the 82nd
> Airborne Division is one of the most elite divisions in the Army … you can be in
> combat within like a week from being notified."

*There were two tours in Korea — one with air-defense artillery as a maintenance
technician, one with the 501st Military Intelligence Group, where his youngest son
was born — and a final posting to Aberdeen, Maryland, home of Army Ordnance. By
then he had been a CW3 for eight years, in the days before there was anywhere
higher to go but a dead man's slot.*

> "I made W3 in 12 years and I stayed W3 all the way for eight more years … You had
> to wait for someone to die or retire or whatever … And so I just couldn't get
> promoted and I just got tired of it told the boss I'm going to have to get out of
> it. Too much politics … You've got to hold your mouth just right and point your
> toes just right. Not the kind of way an 82nd Airborne Division soldier likes to
> be."

*He could have coasted — "ROAD," the old Army joke, Retired On Active Duty — but
coasting was not in him. He retired a Chief Warrant Officer, CW3, on March 1, 1988.
And he left with one promise kept:*

> "I made a promise to myself though That I would never do anything that I'd regret
> later And although I saw a lot of people do things that I know they have to regret
> these days I never did that. **I came back with a clear conscience and still to
> this day**"

## "I'll never be right again"

*Content note: he speaks here, without cover, about what the war left in him.*

> "I've been going through counseling ever since I came back from Vietnam And I've
> never had counseling really do me any good The only thing that really changes is
> we talk about 1968 till now … **I'll never be right again. Never, and neither will
> they.**"

*The "they" were the young soldiers in Iraq — the interview was recorded in 2006,
and he watched the news the way only a combat veteran can:*

> "I don't think that you can support a war and a soldier at the same time. If
> George Bush had to fight the damn war, there wouldn't be a war."

> "If they ever made a law. A national law or an international law that the leaders
> of two countries go into war and be the first people to die in the war there'd
> never be another war"

*Asked how the service shaped his life, he gave both edges of the blade:*

> "One thing if I make up my mind to do something, it'll get done … That is
> something the Army's instilled in me There's hardly any way they're going to stop
> me from doing something I want to do On the other side, I'm a hard individual"

## The soldier becomes the chief

*Retirement terrified him — half the pay, none of the bills cut — until Conway
answered:*

> "I thought that I would go starve to death when I first retired because when you
> retire—I think retirement is like the third or fourth most traumatic thing you can
> do in your life—my military pay would have been cut in half but my bills wouldn't
> be cut in half. … I came down here to Conway applied for every job in the paper
> that I thought I could do. And by the end of the first week, I had so many jobs I
> couldn't even take them all."

*And then came the moment the whole tribe descends from. He bought a company and
went to register it as minority-owned:*

> "When I went to register, they told me that I'd have to be a Lumbee from North
> Carolina because there were no Indians in South Carolina. And I was born and
> raised in South Carolina … It didn't make sense for me to become a Lumbee So I
> sued the state and did paperwork myself Actually, what I did was I filed an old
> lawsuit. And I copied everything and just changed it to [s]ay what I wanted to
> say. And I filed it. And the judge signed the thing and made the state recognize
> my company as a minority-owned company. And that's one of the reasons that I tried
> to organize the Waccamaw Indians because **I didn't think every Indian in the
> state ought to have to fight that battle independently.**"

*That battle is its own exhibit → [The Long Fight](/exhibits/the-long-fight/).*

## The veterans' feather

*Asked for the one thought he wanted future generations to carry, he answered as a
chief:*

> "Whenever we do a powwow, we always give out what we call veterans' feathers
> because we honor the veteran and they can use this veterans feather as a free pass
> to every powwow we do and We realize and we know that **freedom doesn't come
> free.** Somebody has to defend it"

> "We should always make sure that war is the last resort, not the intermediate
> resort as it was in this case."
