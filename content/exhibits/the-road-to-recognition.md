The Waccamaw of the Dimery Settlement near Dog Bluff, South Carolina, never stopped
being a people. What they lacked was *paper* — the legal recognition that unlocks
everything from grants to sovereignty. The archive holds that campaign in documents.

**2000 — the petition.** Resolution **HH-08-08-00-004**, "Petition for Recognition,"
sits at the very start of the modern record, contemporary with the first constitution.
A tribe formally resolving to exist in the eyes of the state.

**2005 — South Carolina says yes.** The Waccamaw Indian People became the **first
tribe recognized by the State of South Carolina** under its recognition process — the
watershed the 2000 petition aimed at. (State recognition is public history; the
archive holds the tribe's side of the correspondence.)

**2007–2013 — the machinery of presence.** Recognition wasn't an ending; it was
admission to the table. The record fills with the follow-through: the Chief appointed
delegate to the state's Native American bodies (JHH-04-23-2007-002, JW-06-01-2007),
a seat on the SC Commission for Minority Affairs advisory committee — held, notably,
by the *retired* Chief (JSH-12-10-2006-001), a federal recognition committee, letters
to Congress (HH-03-04-2010).

**2022 — the federal petition.** Resolution **JHH-01-08-0022-010**: the official
petition for **federal** acknowledgment. That question is still open. The tribe's
support in 2026 for the Schaghticoke Tribal Nation's recognition fight — one of the
last acts of Chief Hatcher's tenure — reads as a people who know exactly how long
that road is, extending a hand to others still walking it.
