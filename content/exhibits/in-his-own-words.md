Nearly every line below is his — verbatim, from the memoir he typed in his own hand
for the Library of Congress, from recorded oral-history interviews, from broadcasts,
and from his last letter to the People. The museum adds only the order, and the
occasional line of connective tissue in *italics*.

## The name

> "Why would two Indians name a kid Harold? That don't make no sense to me! I'd
> rather have the name Buster. I sound like a clown, but, I like it."
> — *WPDE, Jefferson Award profile, 2018*

## The boy

*He grew up in and around Conway and Myrtle Beach in the 1940s and 50s. He set the
whole of it down himself, decades later, for the Veterans History Project.*

> "My family lived mostly in black neighborhoods. That caused problems, but we had
> nothing to compare our lives to, so as far as we knew, everybody lived the same
> way. When I was six, I was enrolled in a white elementary school."

> "The teacher kept asking me if my mom ever washed me or my clothes. She asked me
> if we had any soap at my house and I told her 'no.' … The next day she [gave] me a
> bar of soap to take to my mom."

> "There was only one water fountain in the school. On one of the first days I was
> there, I went and tried to get a drink. I was manhandled about it and told my water
> was outside. I started going to the hose outside to drink."

*Content note: the next passage recounts a racist attack he suffered as a child and,
in his own regretful words, what he said back. The slur is his memoir's, softened
here.*

> "One of them … threw an apple at me over the top of the car. It hit me in the side
> of the face and it hurt. They were yelling at me 'Get out of the road, [n——].' I
> was hurt and scared … so I ran into the diner. Ms. Inez wiped the apple off of my
> face and asked me why I was crying. … I told her (I'm so regretful now but I did
> it) Ms. Inez, I'm not a [n——], if I were a [n——] it would be alright but I'm not …
> You are a [n——]."

*He found one school where the cruelty stopped:*

> "In Conway, I was enrolled in a community school and no one despised me there. I
> went through … Waccamaw Elementary with no hate problems."

*And a caution from his own father, which he spent the rest of his life refusing:*

> "Yes you're an Indian, but you don't need to tell anybody."
> — *his father, recalled to MyHorryNews, 2021*

The line that holds the whole childhood — and the reason the tribe exists:

> "**I was wrong at home because I wasn't dark enough. I was wrong at school because I
> wasn't white enough.**"
> — *Center for Cultural Preservation oral history, 2014*

## Finding home

*He was seven or eight when his mother took him to Cherokee — and in a store there,
an older Cherokee woman treated him as one of her own.*

> "Here was all these people that looked like me… it made me feel like I'm home. This
> is where I'm supposed to be. I have a place to be now. **And that's the way I feel
> about the tribe… it's a place where people that have not been accepted into the full
> society of this country can be accepted.**"
> — *oral-history interview, 2021*

> "I had been an outsider everywhere I was, home or in school or whatever, my whole
> life."
> — *oral-history interview, 2021*

## The soldier

*He typed the war down plainly, the way he lived it.*

> "One day I was driving home and passed an Army recruiting office and decided to join
> the Army. A week later I was on my way to Fort Bragg."

*In Vietnam he was a machine gunner and a tunnel rat with the 82nd Airborne. This is
the action that earned him the Bronze Star — in his own words:*

> "We were down on the third level when the lead man got shot and fell. He had the
> flashlight and whenever I reached for it they would shoot at my hand. The guy behind
> me took off. … I wanted to but I had the urge to bring the lead man with me. I have
> no logical reason but that is how I felt. So, I wrapped my hands into his pant legs
> … and started dragging him a little at a time toward what I thought was the way out.
> … I pulled him out but his head was opened by the shot he took. I didn't know that
> he was dead until I saw him in the light. … I don't know what it was but they gave
> me a Bronze star for it."

*Even the sniper who wounded him got his gallows humor:*

> "I was on guard duty and I smoked … so I was playing and smoking when I got hit. I
> never met the guy who shot me. I suppose it was a sniper firing at my cigarette. It
> might have been because he didn't like Country Music or maybe my version of country
> music."

> "I was evaced to the field hospital … and when I awoke, I had my Combat
> Infantryman's badge, My Bronze Star and my Purple Heart along with a Vietnam Unit
> medal … pinned to my pillow."

*He came home to jeers, not a parade:*

> "We were sent via C-141 to Scott Airforce Base in Illinois. There we were welcomed
> by people calling us names and such."

*And after a jump he'd been ordered to make against medical orders ended his airborne
career, he made a quiet vow:*

> "They classified it as a termination of Airborne status and removed my wings in
> ceremony. **I never put them back on.**"

> "During my military time I got educated and got off of the bottom rung."

*All memoir passages: Library of Congress, Veterans History Project — the Harold
Dalton Hatcher Collection, in his own hand. He retired a Chief Warrant Officer (WO‑3)
in 1988.*

## The people

![Waccamaw Chief Harold Hatcher standing at his father's grave, 1994 — the earliest independent record of him as Chief](/exhibits/gallery/1994-crediford-fathers-grave.jpg)

*Ask him who the Waccamaw were and he'd start two centuries back, at the settlement.*

> "The Waccamaw people came from what is now known as the Dimery Settlement … a
> community of Native American people that … established their own school, their own
> church … and they lived together on that little piece of land for … almost a
> hundred years."

> "In 1813 a fella named John Dimery, which is kin to me … gathered together a bunch
> of his friends, collected some money, and they bought 300 acres of land back from
> Mr. Lewis. And that land is what became the Dimery Settlement."

*His grandfather's fight — the one that runs in the family blood:*

> "In 1935, Horry County decided they … combine the black and Indian school, and they
> told the Indian people to go to the colored school. … So my granddaddy — his name
> was Vander Hatcher — … took them all to the white school, and they wouldn't let them
> in. … a group of white parents came out, beat the kids, and ran them away. So …
> my granddaddy sued the county."

> "They sent a group of learned people out to look at each child individually and
> decide, based on skin color … whether or not they would go to a black school or a
> white school. And we had — one of my uncles went to the black school, and his sister
> went to the white school. **Same mother, same father.**"

*On the name, and the old ways:*

> "Actually the Waccamaw is the name of a river, not a people. … We called it
> Blackwater. Waccamaw. So we became Blackwaters when the white people got here."

> "We didn't have a word that meant hunt. Our word meant making meat, and we felt like
> the only reason to take a life was to sustain life."

*His theology, told through a marble and an anvil:*

> "Everything that is alive is rolled into a big ball or marble. … if you put that
> marble on an anvil and you hit it with a hammer and it flies into a thousand
> different pieces, it doesn't matter how small of a piece it is, and it doesn't
> matter how far away from the anvil it flies. It's always a piece of God, nature."

*And the eagle's feather — the thing the law would not let him have:*

> "Traditionally we like to put an eagle's feather in the casket of all of our dead
> people to lead their spirit back to God. Unfortunately, if you're not a federal
> tribe you can't legally own an eagle's feather. … But my mother died about two years
> ago. She was ninety-eight years old, and **I gave her an eagle's feather.** … Even
> though I'm a combat veteran … I've got a Bronze Star, a Purple Heart, and a
> Vietnamese Cross of Gallantry. And I can't practice the religion of my choice."

> "A four-in-hand tie was a way they used to lead slaves … they could carry four in
> each hand. … I don't wear them now. **I've been a slave long enough, and I'm not
> going to be a slave anymore.**"
> — *all: Center for Cultural Preservation oral history, 2014*

## The fight

> "When I was retired from the Army in 1988, I bought a company and I wanted to
> register it as a minority-owned company. I thought that I had paid the dues. I drank
> out of the colored water fountains. I had sat in the balconies at theaters. I had
> eaten off of paper when other people ate off glass. … But the state of South
> Carolina at that time said there were no Indians in South Carolina, so I ended up
> suing the state and I won."

> "I want it to be that I at least made it a little bit more palatable in South
> Carolina for Indians to be known to have lived here, that we are human beings."

*On the law he changed so a chief could marry his own people:*

> "I had a politician tell me, 'Just go get you a Notary Public seal and you can
> notarize it.' No, I'm not going to do that. **My signature as a chief ought to
> suffice.**"

*On being told what he could and couldn't do, by people who were never there:*

> "Because when I was in Vietnam there were none of these damned politicians there.
> When I got the bullet holes in my belly there was none of them there. And now
> they're telling me what's right and what's wrong? I don't buy that."
> — *oral history, 2014*

> "We don't ask for anything better, but we think we should at least get the same."
> — *WBTW, 2022*

*He never stopped naming the cost of not being federally recognized:*

> "There's 600 sets of human remains now on shelves in South Carolina that we can't
> put back in the ground."
> — *WBTW, 2022*

## The goodbye

> "I have never lied to you, and never stabbed anyone in the back. I do nothing under
> the table… I don't perceive problems other than growing pains, and the disruption
> that any change of command would bring. Please give your loyalty to Chief Cail, I
> believe she will lead ably and honestly… Thank you for allowing me to serve in this
> AWSOME position. I did my best for you."
> — *tribe-wide resignation letter, June 29, 2026, signed "Chief Retired"*

*And the way he ended nearly every conversation — a handshake and an open hand:*

> "Take my hand like an Indian. Good to meet you, sir. If I can help you somehow you
> let me know."
> — *oral history, 2014*
