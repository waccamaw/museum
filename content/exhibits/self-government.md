Most people never see a constitution get written. The Waccamaw wrote theirs **three
times in sixteen years** — 2000, 2008/09, and the **2016 constitution** in force today
— each generation a snapshot of what the tribe had learned about governing itself.
(The 2008/09 rewrite followed the most turbulent chieftaincy years; constitutions get
rewritten when their gaps get found.)

## Three branches

- **Executive** — a Tribal Chief (elected at large, four-year term, genealogical ties
  to the Dimery Settlement required), a Vice Chief elected as running mate, up to two
  appointed Second Chiefs. The Chief runs everything *external* and holds a **veto**,
  overridable only by five of seven Council votes.
- **Legislative** — a seven-seat **Tribal Council** plus an elected Chief of Tribal
  Council. Everything *internal*. Seats staggered against U.S. presidential years.
- **Judicial** — two **Tribal Judges**, an Associate Judge, and a seven-member
  **Council of Elders** who review every ordinance for constitutional conflict and
  run **all elections**.

## The safeguards

- **The three-readings rule** — nothing affecting the membership passes until read
  three times in open meetings. Even waiving the rule takes a resolution — which gets
  its own paper trail.
- **Initiative and referendum** — thirty percent of registered voters can force any
  question onto a ballot, over the government's head.
- **Succession by law, not by fight** — when Chief Hatcher resigned in 2026, the
  office passed to the Vice Chief *automatically*, under Article VII. No interregnum.
- **Votes from anywhere** — online/phone voting rules adopted in 2009; by 2026 the
  Council routinely voted by email, each tally read into the record.

The elegant detail: **Article XI**, the saving clause, keeps every prior resolution in
force "to the extent consistent" with the current constitution — twenty-five years of
tribal law flowing forward through each rewrite. Which is why the tribe keeps a
verified archive of all of it ([about this museum](/exhibits/about-this-museum/)).

## A record that shows its work

The archive keeps the *paper* as faithfully as the law. On the signed originals, the
handwriting is history: an election certification where the tallies are **corrected by
hand** — 69 struck through, 70 written above; a resolution where a confirmation
threshold is amended in ink in the margin; reading dates fixed, months corrected,
"passes" circled. The archive preserves every emendation verbatim, because on
documents like these, the hand-corrections *are* the record of a small government
doing its work honestly.

Constitutional design is usually studied in nation-states. It's worth seeing what it
looks like when a community of a few hundred does it for themselves, carefully, for
a quarter century.
