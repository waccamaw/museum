Between 2021 and 2024, the Waccamaw Indian People and **Coastal Carolina University**
built something together: a public history of the tribe told across a museum gallery,
a walking trail, oral-history recordings, and a website — *Waccamaw Indian People:
Past, Present, Future.* It won a national **Award of Excellence** from the American
Association for State and Local History and the Society for American Archaeology's
award for **Outstanding Public Archaeology Project.** This exhibit preserves what it
made and what it found.

## A tribe and a university, working together

The project grew out of three CCU courses — IDS 430, ANTH 432, and MALS 530 — led by
**Prof. Katie Stringer Clary** (History) and **Prof. Carolyn Dillian** (Anthropology
& Geography), with **thirty-five students** and the tribe's own leaders as partners
and interviewees. It was funded by **South Carolina Humanities**, CCU's Prince
Fellows Program, and others, and made in partnership with the **Horry County
Museum.** Crucially, it was built *with* the tribe, not merely *about* it — the
scholars have written about the collaboration itself in *History News* and *South
Carolina Antiquities*.

> "It is important that information about the pipe be shared orally — not written
> down." — a cultural protocol the project honored, quoted from the exhibit's own
> panel on the ceremonial pipe.

## What it built

- **A museum exhibit** at the Horry County Museum (2021–2022) — with a **3-D virtual
  walkthrough** that lets anyone tour the gallery online, panel by panel.
- **An ecological & cultural interpretive trail** at the Tribal Grounds in Dog Bluff,
  a half-mile loop of outdoor panels, which **opened April 16, 2024.**
- **Oral-history interviews** recorded **March 20, 2021** with Chief Harold "Buster"
  Hatcher, Vice Chief Cheryl Cail, and Second Chief John D. Turner — preserved as
  audio the People can always return to.

## The deep past it recovered

The project reached back long before the written record — the archaeology of the
Waccamaw's ancestors on this coast:

- **Paleoindian** peoples (more than 10,000 years ago), hunter-gatherers of the
  Lowcountry who knapped stone points for game long since vanished.
- **Archaic** shell middens along the coast — clams and oysters, deer taken with the
  atlatl, a spear-thrower that gave a hunter reach and force.
- **Woodland** farmers who raised the **Three Sisters** — corn, beans, and squash
  planted together, each helping the others — and who grew and smoked **tobacco**,
  sharing it with Europeans and, unknowingly, with the world.
- The first Europeans to reach these people were **slavers — Gordillo and Quexos, in
  1521** — who carried off a young man they renamed *Francisco de Chicora*, perhaps
  the first Waccamaw named in any European record. The Spanish called this whole land
  **Chicora.**

## The present it recorded, the future it named

The project let the living tribe speak for itself — nine members, from the Chief to
the fire keeper, on what it means to be Waccamaw today — and it laid out plainly what
recognition still costs. The Waccamaw are **state-recognized (2005), not federally
recognized;** without federal status the tribe cannot repatriate its ancestors under
**NAGPRA**, and a federal recognition bill (**H.R. 1942**, introduced March 2021)
remains unfinished. As one member put it:

> "I am who I am, and no recognition can change that." — Glenn Cook, Chairman of
> Buildings and Grounds
